**Daylight saving time ends Sunday, November 1, 2026, the earliest calendar date it can fall - which drops sunset in Destin from 5:59 in the evening to 4:58 literally overnight, wipes out the after-work walk for anyone who gets home at five, and lands in the exact weeks when Carnegie Mellon researchers found per-mile pedestrian risk jumps by 186 percent, so the honest answer is not "walk in the dark carefully" but "move the work off the sidewalk until people adjust."**

Somebody said this to me at the end of last October and it stuck.

"We finally got past the heat and now it's too dark."

That is the Emerald Coast in one sentence. From roughly June through September the middle of the day is off the table, which is why the [pavement heat tool](/tools/too-hot-to-walk/) exists and why I wrote about [when it is genuinely too hot to walk your dog](/blog/too-hot-to-walk-your-dog/). Then you get maybe six good weeks in October where the evening is finally usable.

Then the clock takes the evening.

## The specific hour you lose

This year is the worst version of it. Daylight saving time ends on the first Sunday in November, and in 2026 that is November 1, the earliest date the rule allows. So the change arrives about as early as it ever can.

Okaloosa County runs on Central time. Here is what that means for Destin in real numbers:

- Saturday, October 31: sunset at 5:59 in the evening
- Monday, November 2: sunset at 4:58
- Monday, November 30: sunset at 4:45

Nothing gradual about it. Saturday you have light until six. Monday you do not. If you get off at five, your usable evening walk window went from about forty-five minutes of daylight to zero, in one weekend.

The mornings do get a compensating hour, and for a small number of people that is a real solution. For most owners, an hour earlier in the morning is theoretical. The dog does not care which explanation you have. It knows the harness stopped coming out.

## Why the first two weeks are the dangerous ones

This is the part I did not know until I went looking, and it changed how I think about November.

Paul Fischbeck and David Gerard, professors at Carnegie Mellon, have studied what happens to pedestrians around the clock change. Their finding: per-mile pedestrian fatality risk jumps roughly 186 percent from October to November, and then falls about 21 percent again by December.

Sit with that shape for a second. If darkness alone were the problem, December would be worse than November, because December is darker. It is not. Risk peaks right after the change and then partly recedes.

Their reading is that the danger is the adjustment, not the dark. Drivers and pedestrians spend a few weeks behaving as though it is still light out at five thirty, because for the last eight months it was. Everybody's habits are one hour out of date at exactly the same time.

The broader picture backs this up. Roughly a quarter of travel happens at night, but something like three quarters of pedestrian fatalities occur in darkness. Research on year-round daylight saving time, including work by Coate and Markowitz published in *Accident Analysis and Prevention*, has estimated that not setting the clocks back would prevent on the order of 171 pedestrian deaths a year in the United States.

You and your dog are pedestrians. In the first two weeks of November, on a residential street, in Florida, you are the least visible you will be all year to a driver who has not recalibrated yet.

## What darkness does to the walk itself

Set the traffic aside for a minute, because the walk gets worse in ways that have nothing to do with cars.

**You lose your read on the dog.** Half of what I do standing next to a working dog is visual. Gait, ear set, tail carriage, where the tongue is. In the dark you have none of it. A limp that starts at minute fifteen is invisible until it is a real limp.

**Reactivity gets worse.** A dog that is [reactive on leash](/blog/dog-reactive-on-leash/) is worse in the dark, because the other dog appears at ten feet instead of forty. There is no runway, no time to create distance, no chance to work under threshold. Every encounter becomes an ambush. A month of ambushes undoes a lot of patient work.

**You cannot see the ground.** Broken sidewalk, storm debris, a dropped chicken bone, glass in a parking lot. All of it is invisible at seven feet.

**The route shrinks.** People stick to lit streets, which means the same short loop every night, which means less distance and less variety just as the dog's outlet is getting squeezed.

So the walk is shorter, more dangerous, less readable, and worse for training. That is four strikes, and most owners respond by doing fewer of them, which is where the real cost shows up.

## The quiet cost of a short winter

Dogs on this coast tend to already be coming off a deconditioned summer. I went through the numbers in [the piece on losing a dog's fitness to the heat](/blog/dog-lost-fitness-over-summer/), and the headline is that in work cited by NC State sports medicine specialist Denis Marcellin-Little, ten dogs whose activity was restricted for eight weeks lost 41 percent of their endurance.

Now stack a dark November and December on top of an already thin September. That is not a seasonal dip. That is most of a year where the dog never gets a real block of consistent work, and it shows up as a dog that is heavier, stiffer, and less capable every spring than it was the spring before.

The behavioral half arrives faster than the physical half. A high-drive dog that goes from a real evening outlet to a lit ten-minute loop starts spending the difference indoors, on your furniture and your evening, which is the mechanism I unpacked in [the post on what under-exercised energy does to a dog](/blog/dog-anxiety-destructive-behavior-exercise/). Most owners notice the behavior in week two and never connect it to the clock.

## Four things that actually work

**Move the session off the evening entirely.** The clock change gives you an hour back in the morning. If your schedule can absorb it, this is the cleanest fix available, and November mornings here are genuinely good, cool and quiet, some of the best walking weather of the year.

**If you do walk in the dark, be visible on purpose.** A light on the dog, not only on you, because from a driver's seat a dark dog four feet ahead of a person is invisible. A reflective or lit collar, a clip light on the harness, a headlamp, and light-colored clothing on yourself. Given what happens to driver behavior in the first weeks of November, treat this as the minimum. Keep the leash short and fixed-length near roads, and keep the dog on the side away from traffic. A well-fitted chest-loading harness like the [Julius-K9 IDC Powerharness](/equipment/julius-k9-idc-powerharness/) gives you a control point that a collar does not, which matters more when you cannot see what is coming.

**Do not use enrichment as the whole answer.** Puzzle feeders and scent games are genuinely useful and they are not a substitute for physical work, for reasons I went into in [mental stimulation versus physical exercise](/blog/mental-stimulation-vs-exercise-dog/). Winter is precisely when people quietly make this trade and then wonder why the dog is climbing the walls by Thanksgiving.

**Move the real work somewhere daylight does not govern.** This is the one that actually solves the problem instead of managing it. Sustained conditioning that does not depend on light, weather, traffic, or how dark your street is means the dog's baseline holds through the whole winter rather than sagging until March.

That last one is most of my November and December. A self-powered mill session in a driveway at six in the evening does not care that the sun set at 4:58. The dog sets the pace, I stand three feet away with a light and can actually read the gait, and the dose is the same on a dark Tuesday as it was in October. That is the practical version of [why structured runs matter](/blog/why-structured-runs-matter/), and why sustained work at a controlled effort beats a scattered short loop, which is the same argument as [how to tire out a high-energy dog](/blog/how-to-tire-out-a-high-energy-dog/).

If you want a number to aim at before you build the winter plan, the [dog exercise calculator](/tools/dog-exercise-calculator/) will give you a defensible daily target by breed group, age, and energy level. Then protect that number through the dark months instead of letting it drift.

## FAQ

**When does daylight saving time end in 2026?** Sunday, November 1, 2026, at 2 a.m. That is the first Sunday in November, which in 2026 is the earliest date the change can fall on, so the evenings get dark about as early in the season as they ever do.

**Is it safe to walk a dog after dark?** It can be, with real visibility and route choices, but the first few weeks after the clock change are the highest-risk window of the year. Carnegie Mellon researchers found per-mile pedestrian risk jumps sharply from October to November and then falls again in December, which points to drivers and walkers adjusting rather than to darkness alone.

**What time does it get dark in Destin in November?** Sunset falls at about 4:58 in the evening on November 2 and drifts to roughly 4:45 by the end of the month. Full dark follows about twenty-five minutes later.

**My dog gets restless in the winter. Is it the shorter days?** Almost always it is the shorter outlet, not the shorter day. When the evening walk shrinks, the energy does not, and it comes out as pacing, chewing, and barking within a week or two. Restore the volume somewhere else and the behavior usually settles.

**Should I switch my dog's exercise to mornings?** If your schedule allows it, yes. The clock change hands you an extra hour of morning light, and November mornings on the Emerald Coast are some of the best conditions of the year. If mornings are not realistic, the answer is to move the work indoors or onto equipment rather than to accept a shorter winter.

## Do not let the winter take the dog's year

You already gave up the middle of the day for four months. You do not have to give up the evenings too.

The owners whose dogs come into spring in better shape than they left fall are not doing anything heroic. They decided in late October where the work was going to happen once the light left, and then they did it.
