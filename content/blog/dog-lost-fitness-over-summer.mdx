**A dog that suddenly gasses out on a walk it used to handle has not gotten lazy or old overnight - it has spent a Gulf Coast summer detrained, losing real cardiovascular and musculoskeletal fitness the same way a human athlete does over an off-season, and the fix is a deliberate four-to-eight week rebuild rather than a return to June's mileage on the first cool morning.**

The first tolerable morning of the fall came in around 74 degrees, and I did what everybody around here does. I grabbed the leash.

Kai made it about ten minutes before the whole picture changed. Shoulders dropping. Tongue wide and flat. That heavy, deliberate trot a dog uses when it is managing effort instead of spending it. This is a Rhodesian Ridgeback mix who runs a slatmill for a living. In May he would have covered that same loop and asked for more.

He was not sick. He was not sandbagging. He was out of shape, and it was my fault for expecting otherwise.

## What four months of Florida actually costs

Here is the part most owners never get told. Fitness is not a trait your dog has. It is an adaptation your dog's body maintains, and it only maintains it because you keep asking.

Denis Marcellin-Little, a veterinary surgeon and sports medicine specialist at NC State, laid this out in his work on conditioning sporting dogs. He cites a study in which ten dogs had their activity restricted for eight weeks. Their endurance dropped by 41 percent. Not their enthusiasm. Their measured endurance, down by nearly half, in two months.

The good news in the same finding is the part I want you to hold onto: those changes were reversible in eight weeks of retraining. The dog is not ruined. The dog is detrained, and detraining runs in both directions.

Now count the calendar on the Emerald Coast. June, July, August, and most of September are functionally unwalkable in the middle of the day, which is exactly why I wrote the piece on [when it is too hot to walk your dog](/blog/too-hot-to-walk-your-dog/) and built the [pavement heat tool](/tools/too-hot-to-walk/) to give you a real number instead of a guess. The walkable window shrinks to a sliver before sunrise and a sliver after sunset. Then the afternoon storms take the evening slot for weeks at a time.

Add it up and a lot of dogs around here just completed a four-month restricted-activity study nobody signed them up for.

## The four systems that quietly gave ground

Detraining is not one thing getting worse. It is four, and they come back at different speeds.

**Cardiovascular.** Sustained work lowers resting heart rate and raises the ceiling on how much work a dog can absorb before it is in trouble. Stop asking, and that ceiling drops. Marcellin-Little also notes that heart rate during exercise runs higher in overweight dogs than in lean ones, which matters because summer usually adds a little weight while it is subtracting fitness.

**Muscle.** The postural and propulsive muscles that hold a dog's gait together over distance are use-it-or-lose-it tissue. A dog carrying less of that muscle in September is a dog whose form falls apart sooner, and form falling apart is where soft tissue injuries live.

**Connective tissue.** Tendon and ligament adapt more slowly than muscle does, which sounds academic until you understand the trap it sets. Your dog's enthusiasm rebuilds in one session. Its muscle rebuilds in a few weeks. Its tendons take longer than that. So a dog that feels great and looks strong can still be running on connective tissue that is three weeks behind the rest of it.

**Paw pads.** This one is pure Florida. Pads condition to the surfaces they meet regularly, and a summer of short grass-only outings at dawn leaves softer pads than a spring of daily sidewalk mileage. The first long pavement walk of the fall lands on tissue that lost its callus.

## The September trap

Here is how this goes wrong, and it is not exotic. It is the most common thing I see.

The weather breaks. The owner is relieved and a little guilty about the summer. So they take the dog out and do a real walk. Forty-five minutes, maybe an hour, pavement, the old route. The dog is thrilled to be out and gives them everything it has, because that is what dogs do.

That night the dog is flat. The next morning it is stiff. By day three the owner is either worried the dog is sick or convinced it is getting old.

None of that is happening. What is happening is a deconditioned athlete got handed an in-season workload, and the bill came due. I have written before about how [a dog can absolutely be over-exercised](/blog/how-much-exercise-does-my-dog-need/), and this is the seasonal version of it. The dose was not crazy in June. It is crazy in September, because September's dog is a different dog.

The cruelest part is what the owner learns from it. They conclude their dog cannot handle much anymore, so they scale down permanently, and the dog spends the fall detraining further from an already low baseline. A four-month gap turns into a year.

## Rebuilding without wrecking anything

The ramp is not complicated. It is just slower than your guilt wants it to be.

**Start at roughly half of what your dog handled in the spring.** Not half of what it can survive today. Half of its old normal. If the spring routine was 40 minutes of real walking, start at 20.

**Add about ten percent a week.** This is borrowed from human endurance coaching for a reason - it is slow enough that connective tissue keeps pace with the enthusiasm. Twenty minutes becomes twenty-two, then twenty-four. It feels like nothing. That is the point.

**Trade one long outing for two short ones.** Two twenty-minute sessions do less damage to an unconditioned dog than one forty-minute session, and on the coast they also let you use both ends of the day while the middle is still hostile.

**Watch the second half, not the first.** Any dog looks good for ten minutes. The information is in minute twenty-five: is the gait still clean, is the head still up, is the dog still choosing the pace. When those slip, the session is over, no matter what the plan said.

**Check the next morning.** Stiffness on the stairs the day after is your dog telling you the dose was too big. Dial back a step and hold there a week.

**Rebuild on forgiving surfaces first.** Grass, packed sand, and dirt before pavement, both for the pads and for the joints. Save the sidewalk mileage for a few weeks in.

If you have no idea what your dog's spring normal actually was, the [dog exercise calculator](/tools/dog-exercise-calculator/) will give you a defensible daily target by breed group, age, and energy level, and the piece on [how much exercise a dog really needs](/blog/how-much-exercise-does-my-dog-need/) explains where those numbers come from. Work backward from the target rather than forward from a number you feel guilty about.

## Where structure beats mileage

The reason I lean on structured conditioning for this exact window is that a rebuild is a dosing problem, and walks are terrible at dosing.

A walk's intensity is set by whatever happens on the walk. A squirrel, a hill, a reactive dog across the street, a stretch of hot sidewalk, your own schedule. The dog gets whatever the route hands it. That is fine when the dog is fit. It is a poor tool when you are trying to add a precise ten percent per week to a body that lost 41 percent of its endurance.

On a slatmill I can hold a pace, hold a duration, watch the gait from three feet away for the entire session, and write down exactly what the dog did so the next session can be exactly a little more. That is the whole argument for [why structured runs matter](/blog/why-structured-runs-matter/), and it is never more true than during a rebuild. The dog controls the speed the entire time, because the belt is self-powered. My job is the dose and the read.

It also solves the surface problem outright, which on a September afternoon in Destin is not a small thing.

One last piece of housekeeping while you are at it. Summers here tend to add a bit of weight along with the deconditioning, and weight changes the math on everything above. Do the hands-on check in the [body condition score tool](/tools/dog-body-condition-score/), or read [the piece on judging whether your dog is overweight](/blog/is-my-dog-overweight/) first. If the answer is yes, tell your veterinarian before you build the plan, not after. I condition dogs. I do not prescribe diets, and anybody who does both without a DVM after their name should worry you.

## FAQ

**How long does it take a dog to lose fitness?** Faster than most owners expect. In the study Marcellin-Little cites, ten dogs lost 41 percent of their endurance after eight weeks of restricted activity. Meaningful loss starts within a few weeks, not months.

**How long does it take to get a dog back in shape?** In that same work, the losses were reversible in eight weeks of retraining. Plan on four to eight weeks of gradual rebuilding to return to a spring baseline, longer for a senior dog or one carrying extra weight.

**My dog seems fine, then is sore the next day. What is that?** That is the classic sign the dose was too large for the current level of conditioning. Dogs mask effort well during the activity and pay for it in the following twelve to twenty-four hours. Cut the session length by a third and hold it there for a week.

**Is it the heat or the fitness making our walks harder?** In September on the Emerald Coast it is usually both, and they are hard to separate by feel. Check the actual conditions with the [pavement heat tool](/tools/too-hot-to-walk/) first. If the numbers are reasonable and the dog still fades early, you are looking at conditioning.

**Does my senior dog need the same rebuild?** The principle is the same and the ramp should be gentler. Older dogs lose conditioning faster and rebuild slower, and any new stiffness deserves a vet visit before a training plan. There is more on this in the post on [exercise for senior dogs](/blog/senior-dog-exercise/).

## The season you actually get back

October through May on this coast is the good stretch. Cool mornings, workable evenings, no pavement that cooks. That is eight months of usable weather sitting right in front of you.

The dogs that make the most of it are the ones whose owners spend September rebuilding on purpose instead of hoping the summer did not count. It counted. Now go take it back.
