**A new puppy that bites, races, and refuses to settle every evening is usually overtired, not under-exercised - young dogs need far more sleep than most homes give them, a tired puppy loses the ability to calm itself, and the fix is enforced naps and short, low-impact activity, not another round of fetch; conditioning is the adult-dog answer, and it starts with a puppy that learned how to rest.**

It is about six thirty in the evening. Dinner is half made. The puppy that was sweet all afternoon has turned into a set of needles attached to a motor. It is biting ankles, then sleeves, then the couch cushion, then ankles again. It sprints a lap around the kitchen island, skids, and comes back for more ankles. Somebody in the house says the thing everybody says.

"He needs to burn it off. Take him out back and throw the ball."

So you do. Ten minutes of fetch. The puppy comes back in more wound up than it went out. By eight you are googling whether something is wrong with your dog.

Nothing is wrong with your dog. It is tired. It is so tired it can no longer act tired.

## A lot of new puppies are about to meet a lot of new owners

Every December a wave of dogs changes homes. Shelters run holiday adoption pushes, breeders time litters for Christmas, and a young dog ends up under a lot of trees on this coast. The part nobody puts on the card is what happens six to eight weeks later. Last Christmas Eve, a Central Kentucky rescue told its local TV station that surrender calls surge right after the holidays, and shelters from North Carolina to New Mexico spent the same month asking people not to give a dog as a surprise gift. Rescues say some version of that every year.

A good share of those dogs are not bad dogs. They are puppies whose owners were never told how much a puppy sleeps, so the evenings turned into a fight, and the fight wore everyone down. If you are one of those owners right now, I want to take something off your plate before we go further. The biting and the chaos are not a sign you picked the wrong dog, and they are not a sign you are doing it wrong. They are a sign the day was missing one thing, and it is a fixable thing.

## How much sleep a puppy actually gets, and how much it needs

The number most veterinary and training sources give for a young puppy is somewhere around 18 to 20 hours of sleep in a 24-hour day. That sounds absurd until you watch a litter at eight weeks - they eat, they wrestle for a few minutes, they fall over where they stand.

Now compare that to what happens in a real house. Dogs Trust, the UK welfare charity, runs a long-term study called Generation Pup that follows thousands of dogs from puppyhood. In 2020 its researchers, working with the University of Bristol, published what owners reported about sleep. At 16 weeks, owners said their puppies slept about 11 hours a day on average. At 12 months, a little under that.

Two cautions on that number. It is what owners reported, and owners miss a lot of naps, so the real figure is probably higher. And averages hide the puppies at the bottom of the range. But even read generously, the gap between what a puppy is thought to need and what a busy household tends to give it is wide. That gap is where the witching hour lives.

## Why an overtired puppy gets louder, not quieter

This is the part that trips everyone up, because it runs backward from how we think tired works.

An adult dog that has worked hard lies down. A puppy that has missed sleep does the opposite. Its brain is still building the machinery that lets a dog notice it is tired and switch itself off. When a young dog runs past its limit, it does not have that off switch yet. What you get instead is arousal stacked on arousal - more biting, more racing, less ability to take a cue it knew fine at noon. Parents of toddlers will recognize the shape of it immediately.

That is why the ball in the backyard fails. The problem was never a surplus of energy the puppy needed to spend. It was a deficit of rest, and the fetch session added more stimulation on top of a brain that was already overloaded. You gave a dog that needed a nap a double espresso.

There is a second reason sleep matters that most owners never hear. In 2017 a team of Hungarian researchers put EEG sensors on dogs after teaching them new commands and recorded their sleep. What happened in the dogs' brains during that sleep was tied to how well they performed on the commands later, and the dogs were better on those commands a week on than they had been right after the lesson. The same basic finding shows up in people: a good share of learning gets locked in while you sleep. For a puppy, that means the sit you practiced at lunch is partly built during the nap after it. Skip the nap and you did half the lesson.

For a long time I read all of that as drive, because Kai is a Rhodesian Ridgeback mix and drive is what those dogs are built on.

I solved half of that problem years before I solved the other half. The adult Kai got his outlet on a slatmill. The puppy Kai needed somebody to put him in a quiet room and shut the door.

## What to do this week

None of this is complicated. Most of it is a schedule.

**Build naps into the day on purpose.** A young puppy should be sleeping after almost every stretch of activity - a meal, a training session, a short outing. For a lot of puppies that means roughly an hour awake, then a real nap, all day long. Do not wait for the puppy to decide. A crate, a pen, or a closed room with a bed and a chew gives it the chance.

**Watch the clock, not the puppy.** By the time a puppy looks tired, you are usually past the point. If the biting ramps up and the puppy has been awake for an hour or more, treat it as a sleep cue, not a play cue.

**Make the evening boring.** The witching hour tends to land between late afternoon and bedtime, which is also when a house is loudest. Get a nap in before it starts. Keep the kids' wrestling and the flirt pole out of that window.

**Swap chase for sniff.** When a puppy does need something to do, scent work and a scatter of kibble in the grass lower arousal instead of raising it. That is the same distinction I made in [the piece on mental stimulation versus exercise](/blog/mental-stimulation-vs-exercise-dog/) - enrichment settles a brain, and a puppy's brain is the thing that needs settling.

**Respect the joints.** Puppies need movement, but not miles. Self-directed play on grass is fine. Forced, repetitive work - long leashed walks on pavement, jogging, stairs - is where the growth plates take a beating. The [puppy exercise planner](/tools/puppy-exercise-planner/) shows what is sensible at each age and adult size, and why the common five-minute rule is a ceiling on forced work, not on play.

**Call your vet if something seems off.** I am not a vet. A puppy that sleeps a lot and wakes up bright is normal. A puppy that is lethargic, not eating, or hard to wake is a vet call, not a schedule problem.

## Where conditioning comes in, and where it does not

People sometimes expect me to say a puppy needs structured exercise. It does not. A puppy needs sleep, short bursts of play it chooses, a little training, and a lot of the world in small doses. Structured conditioning is for a body that is done growing, and your vet is the right person to say when your dog gets there.

What the puppy months do is set the pattern. A dog that learned to switch off at four months carries that into the [adolescent stretch](/blog/dog-adolescence-phase/), which is hard enough without a dog that cannot settle. It carries it into adulthood too. The adult version of the witching hour is the dog that cannot calm down after the [dog park](/blog/dog-park-not-tiring-dog-out/), and the one that chews the house when it is short on real work - I went through that mechanism in [the post on under-exercised dogs](/blog/dog-anxiety-destructive-behavior-exercise/). Both are dogs whose arousal runs ahead of their ability to come down. Sleep is the first tool for that. Honest physical work, later, is the second.

When the time comes, [how much exercise a dog needs](/blog/how-much-exercise-does-my-dog-need/) is the place to start the adult math.

## FAQ

**Why is my puppy so crazy at night?** Most evening chaos in young puppies is overtiredness. A puppy that has not napped enough during the day loses the ability to calm itself, so it bites and races harder instead of lying down. Build naps into the day and get one in before the evening starts.

**How much should a puppy sleep?** Most veterinary and training sources say young puppies need somewhere around 18 to 20 hours in a 24-hour day, dropping as they grow. Owners in a large UK study reported far less than that, which suggests plenty of puppies are running short.

**Should I play fetch to tire out my puppy when it gets bitey?** Usually not. If the puppy has been awake a while, more play adds stimulation to a brain that is already overloaded. A calm spot to nap works better than another round of fetch.

**Is the witching hour a sign of aggression?** Almost never. Mouthing and biting in a tired young puppy is normal development plus overtiredness. If biting is intense at every hour of the day, or comes with stiffening or growling, talk to your vet or a qualified trainer.

**When can my puppy start real exercise?** Short, self-directed play is fine from the start. Longer, forced, repetitive exercise should wait until the growth plates close, which varies by adult size. Ask your vet, and use the puppy exercise planner as a general guide.

## Let the puppy sleep

The best thing you can do for a new puppy this month is protect its naps. It will feel like you are doing less. You are doing the part that matters.
