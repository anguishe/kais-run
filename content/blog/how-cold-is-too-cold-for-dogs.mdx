**General veterinary guidance says most healthy dogs are comfortable at 45 degrees and above, that small, thin-coated, young, old, and sick dogs need real caution below freezing, and that every dog is at risk of cold injury below about 20 degrees - but on the Emerald Coast the more useful answer is that wind, wet fur, a short coat, and a body adapted to heat matter more than the number, and that the cool months are the best stretch of the year to build a dog's conditioning, not a reason to stop.**

On January 21, 2025, the National Weather Service measured 7.5 inches of snow in Fort Walton Beach. Crestview got 9. Niceville got 6. Destin, the town I grew up in, got 2.8. Pensacola recorded 8.9 inches, breaking a snowfall record that had stood since 1895, and dropped to 13 degrees the next morning.

That week, a lot of dogs on this coast met snow for the first time in their lives.

That was a record-breaking storm, not a normal winter. The question it raised comes back every year in a smaller form. A 40-degree morning, a north wind coming off the bay, and a short-coated dog that spent the whole summer panting in the shade. Is it too cold to go out?

Usually, no. But the thermometer is not what decides it.

## The numbers most vets start with

The most common veterinary guidance, laid out by veterinarian Jennifer Coates for PetMD, runs roughly like this. At 45 degrees and above, most dogs are fine. Below 45, some cold-sensitive dogs start to get uncomfortable. Below 32, small dogs, thin-coated dogs, puppies, seniors, and sick dogs should not spend long periods outside. Below 20, every owner should be watching for hypothermia and frostbite.

Notice how much of that depends on the dog. Even the Tufts Animal Care and Condition scale, a screening tool built for animal control officers, reads weather risk on separate bands for small, medium, and large dogs. The same morning is a different morning depending on who is standing in it.

For most of the winter here, that guidance says you are fine. Truly dangerous mornings for a healthy adult dog on this coast are rare. Uncomfortable ones, occasionally risky for the wrong dog in the wrong conditions, are not.

## Why a Florida dog feels it more

**Your dog is adapted to heat, not cold.** PetMD's guidance counts acclimation among the things that change a dog's cold tolerance, and dogs that are regularly exposed to cold do build up tolerance to it. A dog that spent June through September in heavy Gulf Coast heat has had no reason to build any. A 40-degree morning in December is a bigger jolt to that dog than the same temperature is to a dog up north that has been easing into it since October.

**Coat matters more than breed reputation.** Dogs with a double coat carry an insulating undercoat. Short, single-coated dogs like Greyhounds, Whippets, Boxers, Vizslas, and a lot of pit bull mixes lose heat faster. Kai is a Rhodesian Ridgeback mix with a short, tight coat and a body built for heat. On paper, a cold, wet, windy morning is exactly his weak spot.

**Lean dogs carry less insulation.** Body fat insulates. A lean, well-conditioned dog is healthier in every way that counts, and it also carries less insulation than a soft couch dog. That is not a reason to let a dog gain weight. It is a reason to warm a lean dog up properly.

**Size changes the math.** Small dogs have more skin surface for their body mass, so they lose heat faster. A Chihuahua and a Labrador on the same porch are not standing in the same weather.

**Wet and wind do the real damage.** PetMD's guidance points out that a stiff breeze cuts through a coat and that dampness soaking through the fur, a swim included, chills a dog quickly. On this coast that is most of the story. A dry, still 38-degree morning is a brisk walk. A 48-degree afternoon with a north wind and a dog fresh out of the bay is a cold dog.

## The cold risks that actually show up here

Frostbite is not the Emerald Coast's problem. These are.

**Winter swims.** Water pulls heat out of a body far faster than air does. A dog that retrieves in the Gulf or the bay in January and then stands around wet in the wind can chill quickly, even when the air feels mild to you. Keep winter swims short, towel the dog off right away, and get it moving or into the car.

**Older joints.** Cold mornings are often when an arthritic senior looks its stiffest. Keep those outings shorter, let the dog warm up at a walk, and give it a warm, padded place to sleep. If the stiffness is new or getting worse, that is a conversation for your vet, not for me. There is more on keeping an older dog moving in [the senior dog exercise post](/blog/senior-dog-exercise/).

**Cold muscles and hard efforts.** A dog that explodes off the back steps into a full sprint on a 40-degree morning is asking cold tissue for maximum effort. Give it five to ten minutes of walking first, then build. This matters most for lean, high-drive dogs that go from zero to full speed without a second thought.

**Small, young, old, and sick dogs.** These are the dogs the veterinary guidance flags below freezing, and they are the ones that benefit most from a coat on the colder, windier mornings here.

Know the warning signs. PetMD's guidance says shivering, whining, anxious behavior, slowing down, looking for somewhere warm, or holding up a paw means it is time to head inside. Sluggishness, confusion, or severe shivering are signs of hypothermia, and shivering can stop as hypothermia gets worse, so a cold dog that has stopped shivering is not necessarily a warmer dog. Call your vet.

## The best news your dog's conditioning gets all year

For about a third of the year on this coast, the thing that limits how hard and how long a dog can work is heat. Dogs get rid of heat mainly by panting, and panting struggles in thick, hot air. The risk is well documented. A study by researchers at Nottingham Trent University and the Royal Veterinary College, working from the records of more than 900,000 dogs seen by vets in the UK, found that exercise was the trigger in about three quarters of the heat-related illness cases where a cause was recorded. That was the UK. This is the Gulf Coast.

Winter takes that limit away.

December through February is the heart of the stretch when heat is not setting the ceiling on your dog's work. That is the window to build aerobic base, to add the small weekly increases that tendons and pads need weeks to absorb, and to bank fitness before May starts taking it away again. I wrote about [how badly a Gulf Coast summer detrains a dog](/blog/dog-lost-fitness-over-summer/). Winter is where you get ahead of that instead of chasing it every September.

The daily window flips, too. In summer, the middle of the day is off the table. In winter, late morning and early afternoon are usually the warmest hours you get - the same hours the heat took from you in July. If your schedule allows, move the harder work there. If you want sand under the dog, most beaches here are closed to dogs year-round; the [Dog Beach Checker](/tools/dog-beach-checker/) shows the legal beach and trail spots and their winter hours. The dark evenings after the clock change are their own problem, which I covered in [walking the dog after the time change](/blog/dog-walk-dark-after-time-change/).

The owners who waste winter are rarely the ones pushing too hard in the cold. They look at a gray, 45-degree morning, decide it is not a day for it, and repeat that decision all season.

## A cold-morning plan that works

**Warm up at a walk.** Five to ten minutes before anything fast, longer for seniors. The same build-up logic applies to pads and tendons as it does for [a dog training toward a 5K](/blog/can-my-dog-run-a-5k-with-me/).

**Keep the dog moving.** Cold risk climbs when a dog stands still, especially a wet one.

**Dry the dog off.** After a swim, a rain shower, or a romp through soaked grass, towel it down before it sits in the wind or the car.

**Coat the dogs that need it.** Small, thin-coated, young, and senior dogs on the colder, windier mornings. A healthy medium or large dog on a typical winter day here usually does not need one.

**Check the next morning.** Stiffness on the stairs after a cold session means scale back and warm up longer next time.

**Protect the weekly total.** Get a defensible daily target from the [dog exercise calculator](/tools/dog-exercise-calculator/), and hold that number through the season instead of letting gray mornings shave it down. The reasoning behind the number is in [how much exercise a dog really needs](/blog/how-much-exercise-does-my-dog-need/).

This is also where structured work earns its keep in winter. A self-powered slatmill session starts with a real warm-up, the dog sets its pace, and the dose is the same on a raw, windy morning as it was on a mild one, because it does not depend on whether anybody felt like walking. ## FAQ

**How cold is too cold to walk a dog?** General veterinary guidance says most healthy dogs are fine at 45 degrees and above, small, thin-coated, young, old, or sick dogs need caution below 32, and every dog is at risk below about 20. Wind, wet fur, coat type, and size shift those numbers, so watch the dog as well as the forecast.

**Is 40 degrees too cold for my dog?** For most healthy adult dogs, no, especially if the dog is dry and moving. A small, short-coated, senior, or wet dog may be uncomfortable at 40, so keep outings shorter and consider a coat.

**How cold is too cold for a dog to swim?** There is no single safe water temperature, because water pulls heat from a body much faster than air does. In winter, keep swims short, dry the dog off right away, and keep it moving or get it somewhere warm, especially if it is lean or short-coated.

**Do dogs in Florida need a coat in winter?** Most healthy medium and large dogs do not on a typical Emerald Coast winter day. Small dogs, thin-coated breeds, puppies, and seniors can benefit from one on cold, windy, or wet mornings.

**Should I stop exercising my dog when it gets cold?** No. On the Gulf Coast, winter is when heat stops limiting how much work a dog can safely do, which makes it the best time of year to build conditioning. Warm up longer and keep the dog dry.
