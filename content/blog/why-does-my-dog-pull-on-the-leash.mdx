**Most dogs pull on the leash for two reasons that have nothing to do with dominance or rudeness - a comfortable human walking pace sits right between a dog's walk and its trot, so the dog is stuck in a gait it does not want, and every time pulling moves it forward the pull gets paid off - which means the fix is a non-tightening front-clip harness, consistent loose-leash training, and a separate outlet where the dog gets to move at its own speed.**

January is Walk Your Dog Month. The idea is a good one. Get outside, thirty minutes a day, every day for a month. The problem is that for a lot of owners the walk is the worst half hour of the day. The dog hits the end of the leash in the driveway and stays there. Your shoulder gets a workout it did not ask for. The dog wheezes against its collar for two miles. You come home annoyed and the dog comes home no calmer than it left.

So the walks get shorter. Then some days they do not happen. And the dog that pulls because it needs to move ends up moving less.

Here is what I wish someone had told me years ago. Your dog is not trying to be in charge. It is dealing with a speed problem and a reward problem, and you can fix both.

## The speed problem nobody mentions

Dogs have three main gaits. A walk, a trot, and a gallop. People have two. That difference matters more than it sounds.

A researcher named Janusz Blaszczyk published a study in 2001 that followed 22 dogs moving freely over long distances and measured exactly when they switched gaits. Depending on size, the dogs walked up to roughly 2 to 2.7 miles an hour, and then switched to a trot. And through most of the trials, given the choice, the dogs trotted. The trot is a dog's efficient, all-day travel gait. It is the one that covers ground cheaply.

Now look at the person on the other end of the leash. A comfortable adult walk is usually put at around 2.7 to 3.1 miles an hour. That is faster than most dogs want to walk and slower than they want to trot. You are asking the dog to hold a pace that sits right in its gear change. The dog tries the trot, because the trot is comfortable. The trot is faster than you. The leash goes tight.

Watch Kai on a long, open stretch with nothing to hold him back. He does not walk. He trots, and he holds it. Ridgebacks were bred to cover ground for hours at exactly that gait. Put that dog on a six-foot leash next to a man walking to the mailbox, and of course he is out front.

That is not an excuse for pulling. It is the reason pulling shows up in almost every dog, including the well-trained ones, and it tells you something useful. A walk at your pace was never going to be the dog's workout. It is a different activity.

## The reward problem

The second half is plain learning.

When a dog pulls and the pull gets it to the next smell, the next tree, the next dog, the pull worked. It got paid. Do that on every walk, hundreds of steps a day, and you have run the most consistent training program your dog has ever had. The lesson is: tight leash means we go.

This is why "he knows heel, he won't do it on walks" is such a common line. The dog has learned two rules. Heel means heel for the length of the command. The rest of the time, the leash is a lever.

## What the harness research actually says

Harness versus collar is the first thing owners argue about, and the research is more interesting than the argument.

In 2021, researchers from the University of Queensland and RSPCA Queensland tested 52 shelter dogs with a leash tension meter, once on a flat collar and once on a back-clip harness. When the dogs were pulling toward food, they pulled harder and for a larger share of the time in the harness. When the lure was a toy, there was no real difference.

It is tempting to read that as a win for collars. It is not. A back-clip harness makes pulling more comfortable, so a dog in one has less reason to stop. A collar puts all that force on the neck. In 2025, Camila Cavalli and Alexandra Protopopova of the University of British Columbia's Animal Welfare Program reviewed the research on collars, harnesses, and head collars. They pulled together measurements showing collars can put very high pressure on a small area of the neck, and one study they cite found pressure inside the eye rose far more when a dog pulled into a collar than into a harness. That matters for any dog, and a lot for a flat-faced breed or a dog with eye problems.

Their recommendation for a dog that pulls runs from least invasive to most. A non-tightening front-clip harness first, where the leash attaches at the chest and turns the dog slightly when it leans in. A martingale collar next. A head collar only as a last resort.

For conditioning work I use a back-clip harness, the [Julius-K9 IDC Powerharness](/equipment/julius-k9-idc-powerharness/), because on a slatmill the dog is not pulling against a person - it is moving at its own pace with the harness there as a safety point. That is a different job from a walk. For a dog that drags you down the street, the front clip is where I would start.

## How to fix it, in order

I am not going to pretend a blog post replaces a good trainer. If your dog is strong enough to put you on the ground, or if the pulling turns into lunging at other dogs, get help in person. That second one is often a different problem entirely, and I covered it in [the post on leash reactivity](/blog/dog-reactive-on-leash/).

For ordinary pulling, this is the sequence.

**Change the equipment first.** A well-fitted non-tightening front-clip harness reduces pulling without leaning on the neck. It does not teach anything. It buys you a walk you can train on.

**Stop paying off the pull.** When the leash goes tight, you stop. When it goes slack, you move. Every single time, for the whole walk, for weeks. The consistency is the entire method. If you only do it when you have patience, you are running two rule sets and the dog will keep testing which one is on.

**Pay the position.** A treat at your leg when the leash is loose teaches the dog where the good stuff happens. Early on, pay often.

**Let the dog sniff on cue.** A good chunk of pulling is the dog trying to get to information. Give it structured sniff breaks and the pressure to drag you toward every mailbox drops. Enrichment matters here for the same reasons I laid out in [the piece on mental stimulation](/blog/mental-stimulation-vs-exercise-dog/).

**Walk a tired dog, not a full one.** A dog that walks out the door with a full tank has more reason to pull and less patience for training. If the walk is the dog's only outlet all day, you are trying to teach loose-leash manners at the exact moment the dog is least able to learn them.

## Where conditioning comes in

That last point is the one most loose-leash guides skip, and it is where the speed problem comes back.

If the walk is the only exercise your dog gets, then the walk has two jobs that fight each other. It has to be a calm, structured training walk at your pace. It also has to be the dog's only chance to move at its own. It cannot be both. The dog wants to trot. You need it to walk. Every outing turns into a negotiation.

Split the jobs and the negotiation goes away. The dog gets a separate outlet where it moves at its own pace - a long line in an open field, a run, or a self-powered [slatmill](/blog/is-a-slatmill-safe-for-dogs/) where the dog sets the speed and the belt follows it. Then the walk can go back to being a walk. I laid out that difference in [the slatmill versus walk comparison](/blog/dog-treadmill-vs-walk-comparison/), and the broader case for building the outlet first is in [how to tire out a high-energy dog](/blog/high-energy-dog-breeds-exercise-guide/).

If you are not sure how much work your dog needs before the walk is a fair fight, the [dog exercise calculator](/tools/dog-exercise-calculator/) gives you a defensible daily number. And if you want to take the walk seriously, [training your dog up to a 5K](/blog/can-my-dog-run-a-5k-with-me/) is a good way to give the trot somewhere to go.

January on this coast helps too. It is the one month you can walk at any hour without watching the pavement temperature, though the early dark is its own issue, which I covered in [the post on walking after the time change](/blog/dog-walk-dark-after-time-change/).

## FAQ

**Why does my dog pull on the leash?** Mostly because a human walking pace is awkward for a dog, which would rather trot, and because pulling usually works - the dog gets where it wants to go. Pulling is learned and reinforced, not a sign the dog is trying to dominate you.

**Does a harness make a dog pull more?** A back-clip harness can make pulling more comfortable, and one 2021 study found dogs pulled harder toward food in a back-clip harness than in a collar. That is not an argument for a collar. For dogs that pull, a 2025 University of British Columbia review recommends a non-tightening front-clip harness first.

**Is a collar or a harness better for a dog that pulls?** For most dogs that pull, a non-tightening front-clip harness. Collars concentrate force on the neck, and pulling into one raises pressure inside the eye far more than a harness does, which matters for flat-faced breeds and dogs with eye conditions.

**How long does it take to teach loose-leash walking?** It depends on the dog and on how consistent you are. Most owners see progress in a few weeks once they stop moving forward on a tight leash every time, not only when they remember.

**Will more exercise stop my dog from pulling?** Not by itself, because pulling is a learned habit. But a dog that gets a real outlet before the walk pulls with less urgency and learns faster, which makes the training work.

## Give the walk one job

Your dog is not being rude. It is stuck between gears, and it has been paid to pull for as long as it has worn a leash. Fix the equipment, stop paying the pull, and give the dog somewhere else to move at its own speed.
