**You build muscle on a dog the same way you build it on a person - by asking it to do a little more work than it is used to, a little harder, on a schedule with rest built in - which means hills, controlled strength work like sit-to-stands and stepping over obstacles, and sustained trotting, not more miles at the same easy walking pace; and if the muscle loss is new, sudden, or on one side, that is a vet visit before it is a training plan.**

The message usually goes something like this. "His back legs look skinny. He used to be solid through the hips. We walk him every day, twice a day. What are we doing wrong?"

Nothing is wrong with the walks. They are just the wrong tool for this job. A daily walk at the same pace, on the same flat loop, is maintenance. It tells the body that what it already has is enough. It does not ask for anything new, so the body does not build anything new. And maintenance has a way of turning into slow loss, because age, a lighter summer, or a bad month quietly lowers the bar and nothing pushes it back up.

I am not a veterinarian. Everything below assumes a dog your vet has looked at and cleared for more work. Hold onto that, because the first section of this post is about when not to start.

## First, figure out what kind of muscle loss you are looking at

Muscle loss in a dog comes in two very different flavors, and they call for different first moves.

The slow kind creeps in over months. The dog is a little narrower through the thighs, the spine and hip bones feel more obvious under your hand, and it happens on both sides evenly. This is the kind that follows a lazy season, a weight-loss diet done without enough activity, or plain aging. Researchers at the University of Naples looked at muscle biopsies from 25 geriatric dogs and found clear signs of muscle atrophy compared with young dogs. Age takes muscle unless something pushes back.

The fast kind is different. Muscle that disappears over a few weeks, loss on one side more than the other, a dog that sits crooked, knuckles a paw, drags toes, or struggles to get up - those are signs of pain, an orthopedic problem, or a neurological one. A dog favoring a sore knee stops loading that leg, and the unloaded leg shrinks. You can build muscle on top of an injury, but it is the wrong order of operations. Get the diagnosis first. Then build.

One more thing worth knowing before you judge by eye. Muscle loss hides under fat. A dog can hold the same weight on the scale while trading muscle for fat, and look fine from across the room. Veterinary nutritionists use a muscle condition score for this, separate from the body condition score, by feeling over the spine, shoulder blades, skull, and hips. A 2019 study led by Lisa Freeman at Tufts tested that score in 40 dogs and found raters agreed well on normal muscle and severe loss, and much less on mild and moderate loss. In other words, early muscle loss is hard to see even for trained hands. If you think you are seeing it, you are probably late. I went into why weight misleads in [the piece on whether your dog is overweight](/blog/is-my-dog-overweight/).

## Why more walking does not build muscle

Muscle responds to load it is not used to. That is the whole mechanism, in dogs and people. Ask a muscle to produce more force or to work longer than its current baseline, give it time to recover, and it adapts by getting stronger and bigger. Ask it for the same thing it did yesterday, and it has no reason to change.

A walk is mostly a low-force, repetitive task. A healthy dog can walk for hours, which tells you how far under its ceiling that effort sits. Add a second walk and you have doubled the time spent below the threshold that drives adaptation. The dog gets a little fitter aerobically at first, then plateaus.

The research on which movements load the hind end is useful here. A 2019 University of Tennessee study by Hannah McLean, Darryl Millis, and David Levine put sensors on the thigh and hip muscles of 12 healthy dogs and compared common rehab exercises against a plain walk. Trotting, stepping up and over an obstacle, and moving backward all drove more muscle activity than walking. Stepping up and over an obstacle lit up the big front-of-thigh muscle more than anything else they tested.

They also tested ankle weights, the thing a lot of owners reach for first. Adding weight to the leg at a walk actually lowered muscle activity, likely because the dogs changed how they moved to compensate. More weight is not automatically more work.

Hills matter too. Researchers at the University of Cordoba in Spain measured muscle activity in greyhounds walking and trotting on flat ground, uphill, and downhill. Going uphill raised activity in the gluteal muscles and the long muscles of the back. Downhill lowered it. Uphill is where the rear engine works hardest, because climbing shifts the dog's weight back onto the hind legs.

So the formula is not complicated. Trot instead of walk. Climb instead of cruise. Add controlled strength work. Then rest.

## A plan that builds muscle without breaking the dog

Here is the order I would work in, for a healthy adult dog your vet has cleared.

**Start with a baseline.** Put your hands on the dog and note what you feel over the spine, the shoulder blades, and the hips. Take a photo from above and from the side in the same spot and the same light. Muscle changes slowly, and your memory will not be reliable over six weeks. A tape measure around the widest part of the thigh, taken the same way each time, helps.

**Swap some walking for trotting.** A dog's trot is its efficient, sustained working gait, and it loads the hind end more than a walk. Kai trots when he has open ground in front of him and no reason to hold back. That sustained trot is the gait most conditioning is built on. On a leash, that means you jog, bike, or use a long line in an open space. Build the duration slowly. Pads and tendons adapt more slowly than lungs, which is the same lesson from [training a dog up to a 5K](/blog/can-my-dog-run-a-5k-with-me/).

**Find a hill.** This coast is flat, which is part of why so many local dogs are softer through the rear than they should be. Bridges with pedestrian paths, the grades in the state forest trails, the ramps and slopes at parks, even a long driveway incline will work. Walk up at a steady pace, keep the dog moving straight, and walk down slowly. Uphill is the strength work. Downhill is where you stay careful.

**Add controlled strength work, two or three times a week.** Sit-to-stands on a flat, grippy surface, where the dog sits square and stands without sliding its feet forward. Stepping over a row of low poles or broomsticks laid on the ground, at a walk, so the dog has to lift each foot deliberately. Backing up a few steps on cue. Standing on a slightly unstable surface for short holds, if your dog is steady. Five to ten clean reps of each, stopped before the form falls apart. Quality is the whole point. A sloppy sit-to-stand where the dog kicks a leg out to the side is teaching it to avoid the work.

**Use steady, sustained work for the engine.** The trot builds the working muscle. A self-powered [slatmill](/blog/is-a-slatmill-safe-for-dogs/) is one way to get sustained trotting that the dog controls, because the belt only moves when the dog drives it, and a dog pushing its own belt is loading its hind end on every stride. I condition Kai on one in a Julius-K9 harness. The mill is not required. Consistency is. If you are weighing whether a mill makes sense for your dog, I laid out the questions in [is a dog treadmill worth it](/blog/is-a-dog-treadmill-worth-it/) and the safety side in is a slatmill safe for dogs.

**Rest on purpose.** Muscle is built during recovery, not during the work. Hard days followed by easy days. Strength work on non-consecutive days. A dog that is stiff the next morning, reluctant on stairs, or quietly skipping its usual jump onto the couch is telling you the load went up too fast. I wrote the full list of warning signs in [can you over-exercise a dog](/blog/how-much-exercise-does-my-dog-need/).

**Progress slowly.** Add one thing at a time. A few more minutes of trotting, or a couple more reps, or a slightly steeper hill. Not all three in the same week. Give each step about a week or two before adding the next.

**Talk to your vet about food.** Muscle needs protein and enough total energy to build with. That does not mean you should start adding supplements or changing the diet on your own. Your vet can tell you whether the current food fits a dog that is now working harder, especially if the dog is also losing weight.

## Seniors need this more, not less

The owners who most need this post are often the ones most afraid to use it. The dog is getting older, the back legs are thinning out, and the instinct is to protect it with rest.

For most healthy seniors, that instinct speeds up the loss. Muscle is what holds a joint in place. Lose it, and the joint takes more of the load, which hurts, which makes the dog move less, which costs more muscle. Gentle, progressive work breaks that loop. The plan above scales down: shorter hills, fewer reps, more rest days, softer surfaces. I made the case at length in [the senior dog exercise post](/blog/senior-dog-exercise/).

January on the Emerald Coast is a good time to start. It is the one stretch of the year when the heat is not setting the schedule, and I covered what the cold actually means for local dogs in [how cold is too cold](/blog/how-cold-is-too-cold-for-dogs/). Six to eight weeks of consistent work from now, before the heat comes back, is enough time to see a real change under your hand.

## FAQ

**How do I build muscle on my dog's back legs?** Load the hind end a little more than it is used to, then let it recover. Uphill walking and trotting, sit-to-stands, and stepping over low poles all work the rear muscles harder than a flat walk, according to university studies that measured muscle activity. Build up over weeks, with rest days between strength sessions.

**Why is my dog losing muscle in its back legs?** Slow, even loss over months is often age, weight loss, or a drop in activity. Fast loss, loss on one side, or loss with limping, crooked sitting, or dragging feet can mean pain or an orthopedic or neurological problem. Any of those is a vet visit before an exercise plan.

**Can walking build muscle on a dog?** Walking maintains muscle, but it rarely builds it in a healthy adult dog, because it is well below the dog's working capacity. Trotting, hills, and controlled strength exercises are what add load.

**Do ankle weights help a dog build muscle?** Not reliably. A 2019 University of Tennessee study found that adding leg weights at a walk lowered muscle activity, likely because the dogs changed their gait to compensate. Hills and step-overs are a safer bet.

**How long does it take to build muscle on a dog?** Expect weeks, not days. With consistent work two or three times a week and real rest in between, most owners feel a difference within six to eight weeks. Take photos and measurements at the start so you can see it.

## Build it before you need it

Muscle is the thing that keeps a dog sound, stable, and moving well into old age. It does not come from doing the same walk more often. It comes from asking a little more, on purpose, and then letting the body catch up.
