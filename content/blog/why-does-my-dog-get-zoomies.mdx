**Dogs get the zoomies - short, frantic bursts of running that veterinarians call frenetic random activity periods - to release built-up energy or stress, and they are normal and usually harmless; but a dog that zooms every single night is showing you that its day stored more arousal than it spent, which means the fix is not stopping the zoomies, it is a real outlet earlier in the day, plus a safe surface for the laps that still happen.**

It is nine o'clock. The dishes are done. Somebody is finally sitting down. And the dog, who has been asleep on the rug for an hour, stands up, drops its hips, tucks its tail, and goes. Couch, hallway, kitchen, couch. The rug ends up in the kitchen. Something falls off the coffee table. Ninety seconds later the dog flops down, panting and pleased with itself, and the house goes quiet again.

Almost every owner has seen it. Most laugh about it, and they should. It is one of the most honest things a dog does. But the question people actually type into a search bar is "why," and the answer is more useful than "dogs are weird."

## What zoomies actually are

The technical name is frenetic random activity periods, usually shortened to FRAPs. Cornell's College of Veterinary Medicine describes them as episodes of wild running, often with the rear tucked under and fast laps around a room or yard. Cornell is also straightforward about what is known: there is no single identified cause. The working explanation from veterinary behavior specialists is that zoomies release pent-up energy, or relieve stress, or both.

They are common in young dogs and still show up in adults and seniors. They usually last a few minutes or less and end on their own. And they tend to happen at predictable moments. Cornell's list includes an owner coming home, the evening, during play or training, after the dog goes to the bathroom, and around exciting events. Anyone who has bathed a dog can add one more.

None of that is a problem. A dog having a quick, joyful run around a safe space is a dog doing exactly what its body was built to do.

## The timing is the useful part

Here is where I think most zoomie articles stop too early.

A release valve opens when pressure builds. Watch when your dog zooms and you learn where the pressure came from.

**After a bath or grooming.** This is the dog shaking off a stressful few minutes of being held still, wet, and handled. It is a stress release, and it says nothing about the dog's exercise.

**When you walk in the door.** Arousal from the reunion has to go somewhere, and a dog that has been alone for eight hours has plenty of it stored. A dog that zooms at every homecoming is often a dog that did nothing with its day but wait.

**After the bathroom or after a meal.** Short, common, and mostly a sign of a dog that feels good.

**Every night, around the same time.** This is the one worth paying attention to. A dog that does laps at nine o'clock almost every evening is spending, in ninety seconds, energy that the day never asked for. The walk was a stroll. The yard time was standing around. Nothing in the day took the dog to the level of effort its body was built for, so the body finds its own way to get there, in the living room, at the worst possible time.

That nightly pattern is the same pressure that shows up elsewhere as chewing, pacing, and not settling. I laid out that mechanism in [the post on destructive behavior](/blog/dog-anxiety-destructive-behavior-exercise/). Zoomies are the friendly version of it. It is the dog handling the problem itself, which is better than the dog handling it with your couch cushions.

## Puppies are a special case

If the dog doing laps is a young puppy and the time is early evening, the answer is often the opposite of "more exercise." Puppies that have been awake too long get wilder, not sleepier, and a lot of so-called zoomies in the first months are a puppy running past the point where it should have been asleep. I wrote about that in [the post on overtired puppies](/blog/overtired-puppy-witching-hour/). The fix there is protected naps, not a harder play session.

For an adult dog, especially a high-drive one, the logic runs the other way. The body is ready for work. It is not getting enough.

## What to change

**Do not try to stop the zoomies.** Chasing a zooming dog turns ninety seconds into a game of keep-away, and scolding it teaches nothing useful. If the space is safe, let it run and let it finish.

**Make the laps safe.** Cornell flags the real risk plainly: it is not the zoomies, it is what the dog runs into. Stairs, slick floors, furniture corners, and roads are where zoomies turn into injuries. Hardwood and tile are the worst offenders. A dog cornering at full speed on a slick floor can strain a knee or a shoulder in a single turn. Rugs with grip pads on the main lap path help. So does moving the evening wind-down to a room with carpet, or letting the dog out into a fenced yard when you see the hips drop. Never let a zooming dog near an open door or an unfenced yard next to a road.

**Move the effort earlier.** If the nightly laps are the pattern, the day needs a real outlet. Not a longer stroll. Sustained, steady work at a real effort level, the kind I described in [how to tire out a high-energy dog](/blog/high-energy-dog-breeds-exercise-guide/). For a lot of dogs that is twenty to thirty minutes of trotting or running rather than sixty minutes of walking. And it matters when it happens. Work in the late afternoon tends to take the edge off the evening better than work at six in the morning.

**Add the brain work, but do not stop there.** Scent games and food puzzles lower a dog's arousal and help it settle, and they are worth doing every day. They do not replace the physical piece. I covered why in [the post on mental stimulation versus exercise](/blog/mental-stimulation-vs-exercise-dog/).

**Skip the dog park as the fix.** It feels like the obvious answer. Often it adds arousal instead of spending it. Short sprints, long pauses, lots of social pressure, and a dog that comes home more wired than it left. I explained that pattern in [the dog park post](/blog/dog-park-not-tiring-dog-out/).

**Mind the surface outdoors, too.** Cornell lists sand and gravel alongside ice and slick floors as slip risks. Zoomies on the beach look great in a video. They are also how dogs tweak a wrist on soft sand. And on this coast, the beach question comes first anyway, because most of the public sand here is off limits to dogs. ## When zoomies are not zoomies

Cornell's line on when to worry is a good one. Happy zoomies look loose. Play bows, a wiggly body, a relaxed face. A dog that runs in a frantic way while looking tense, wide-eyed, and tightly tucked, or that seems to be running away from something, may be frightened or in pain. Repetitive circling that the dog cannot be interrupted from, spinning after its tail for long stretches, or bouts that come with confusion, stumbling, or collapse are not zoomies. Those are a call to your veterinarian. I am not one, and none of that is something to diagnose at home.

## FAQ

**Why does my dog get zoomies at night?** Evening zoomies usually release energy or arousal the day did not spend. A dog that does it now and then is normal. A dog that does it every night is often telling you it needs more real exercise earlier in the day.

**Are zoomies normal in adult dogs?** Yes. They are most common in young dogs, but adult and senior dogs get them too. The usual concern is not the zoomies themselves but the surface and obstacles the dog runs into.

**Why does my dog get zoomies after a bath?** Most likely as a stress release after being held still, wet, and handled. It is common, short, and not a sign that anything is wrong with the dog's exercise.

**Should I stop my dog's zoomies?** Usually no. Make the space safe and let the dog finish. Chasing it turns the zoomies into a game and makes them last longer. If they happen every night, change the day, not the zoomies.

**When should I worry about my dog running in circles?** If the dog looks frightened or in pain, cannot be interrupted, spins after its tail for long periods, or seems confused or unsteady, call your veterinarian.

## Read the valve, change the day

Zoomies are one of the best things a dog does. They are also data. Once a week after a bath is a dog shaking off a bad five minutes. Every night at nine is a dog showing you what its day was missing.
